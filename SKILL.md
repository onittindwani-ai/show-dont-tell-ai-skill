# Show, Don't Tell

## Why this skill exists

Almost every business explains itself the same way:

> "We are a leading provider of high quality solutions with a customer first approach. We offer a wide range of services. Feel free to contact us for more information."

A competitor could paste that exact paragraph on their own site and it would still be true. That's the problem. Text that could belong to anyone gets remembered by no one. The reader can't picture anything, can't check anything, and doesn't know what to do next, so they leave.

People don't buy descriptions. They buy a result they can picture, from someone they believe, with an easy first step. That is the whole method:

1. **Show the result** (what changes for the customer), not the product.
2. **Show the change** (before and after), not the features.
3. **Show the evidence** (real proof), not adjectives.
4. **Show the door** (one clear next step), not "contact us".

Your job: take what the person gives you and rewrite it so a busy stranger understands in about 5 seconds what they get, why they should believe it, and exactly what to do next. You never invent anything to get there.

## The 6 rules (never break these)

### Rule 1. Result first

The first thing the reader sees is what **they** get, in **their** words, not what the business is.

- Bad: "ABC Dental is a modern multi specialty clinic."
- Good: "Walk out with a whiter smile in 1 visit."
- Bad: "We are a full service digital marketing agency."
- Good: "More booked calls from your website, every week."

How to find the result: ask "so what?" about the business's description until you hit something the customer would feel, count or tell a friend.

> "We do bookkeeping." So what? "Your books are always up to date." So what? "You never lose a weekend to receipts again, and tax time takes 1 call." That's the result.

The result must be specific (a thing, a time, a number, a moment), true for this business, and something the customer actually wants (not what the owner is proud of).

### Rule 2. Before and after

Show the customer's world before and after, in 1 or 2 short lines each. Make it concrete and physical: a moment, a feeling, hours, money, a place, an object.

- Weak: "Before: inefficient processes. After: optimised workflows."
- Strong: "Before: you copy every order into 3 spreadsheets by hand at 11 pm. After: it's done before you finish your coffee."

Good before and after lines pass the **camera test**: you could film them. "Peace of mind" can't be filmed. "You stop checking your phone during dinner" can.

### Rule 3. Real proof only

Use only proof the person gave you or that already exists in their text or materials: a real number, a real review (word for word), a real client result (with permission), a real count, a guarantee they truly offer, a licence or award they truly hold, or a visible process detail.

**Never invent** a number, percentage, review, client name, logo, rating, award, years in business, guarantee or "as seen in". Never round up. Never turn one client's result into a promise for everyone.

When proof is missing, put a marker exactly where it belongs: `[PROOF NEEDED: a short review from a recent customer about speed]`. Then tell the person exactly how to collect it (see [references/PROOF-RULES.md](references/PROOF-RULES.md)). A marker is honest and fixable. Fake proof is a lie that destroys trust the moment someone checks, and in many countries it is illegal.

### Rule 4. One clear next step

End with exactly **one** action. It should be specific (what to do), small (low effort, low risk), and say what happens next.

- Bad: "Feel free to contact us for more information."
- Bad: "Call us, email us, follow us on Instagram, or visit our store!"
- Good: "Book a free 15 minute call. You'll leave with a price and a start date."
- Good: "Reply YES and I'll send the 1 page plan for your shop today."

See the next step library in [references/NEXT-STEPS.md](references/NEXT-STEPS.md).

### Rule 5. Plain words

Short sentences. Everyday words a 12 year old knows. One idea per sentence. Cut every phrase in [references/EXPLAINING-PHRASES.md](references/EXPLAINING-PHRASES.md). Replace adjectives with facts: not "fast delivery" but "delivered in 2 days".

### Rule 6. Keep it true, and keep it theirs

Keep the person's facts, prices, services, names, offer and promises exactly. Do not add services they don't sell or guarantees they didn't offer. Match their tone (warm, formal, playful, local language). Keep the length close to the original unless they ask otherwise. You are making their truth clearer, not making up a better business.

## How to work, step by step

### Step 1. Understand what you've been given

Identify the type of text. Each type has a template:

| Type | Template |
| --- | --- |
| Website homepage, landing page, service page | [templates/HOMEPAGE.md](templates/HOMEPAGE.md) |
| Cold email, sales email, follow up | [templates/SALES-EMAIL.md](templates/SALES-EMAIL.md) |
| Proposal, quote, pitch deck opening | [templates/PROPOSAL-OPENER.md](templates/PROPOSAL-OPENER.md) |
| WhatsApp, Instagram DM, email reply to an enquiry | [templates/DM-REPLY.md](templates/DM-REPLY.md) |
| Google Business profile, directory listing, marketplace listing | [templates/GOOGLE-PROFILE.md](templates/GOOGLE-PROFILE.md) |
| Elevator pitch, intro at a meeting, video intro, bio | [templates/PITCH.md](templates/PITCH.md) |

If the type is not obvious, ask **once**: "Is this your homepage, a sales email, a proposal, a reply to an enquiry, your Google profile, or a pitch?" If it is obvious, don't ask.

Also notice: who the customer is, what the business actually sells, the price if given, the location if it matters, and the tone.

### Step 2. Audit the original (silently, or shown if they asked for a review)

Score it with [references/AUDIT-SCORECARD.md](references/AUDIT-SCORECARD.md): 6 areas, 0 to 5 each, 30 points total. Note:

- Every explaining line (quote it).
- The real result hiding in the text, if there is one.
- Every piece of real proof already present.
- The next step, and whether there's one, none, or too many.

If the person asked "what's wrong with my copy?", show the score and the top 3 fixes before rewriting.

### Step 3. Collect proof

List the proof already in the text. If there is none, ask **once**, briefly:

> "To make this believable, do you have any of these? Paste anything you have:
> 1. A number (customers served, years, jobs done, a result like 'saved 6 hours a week')
> 2. A short review from a real customer
> 3. A before and after from one job
> 4. A guarantee you really offer
> If you have none yet, say 'none' and I'll mark the spots and show you how to collect them."

If they say none or don't answer, continue with `[PROOF NEEDED: ...]` markers. Never stall the whole job waiting for proof.

### Step 4. Find the result and the change

Use the "so what?" ladder (Rule 1) and the camera test (Rule 2). If there are several results, pick the **one** the customer cares about most (usually: save time, make money, avoid a pain, look good, feel safe). Use the industry guide in [references/INDUSTRY-PLAYBOOKS.md](references/INDUSTRY-PLAYBOOKS.md) for ideas, but only claim what is true for this business.

### Step 5. Write the headline

Write 5 headline options using different formulas from [references/HEADLINE-FORMULAS.md](references/HEADLINE-FORMULAS.md), then pick the best. Keep it 4 to 10 words. Show the person the winner plus 2 alternatives.

### Step 6. Rewrite with the template

Follow the matching template line by line. Keep their facts. Use their proof. Mark missing proof. End with one step.

### Step 7. Check yourself

Run through the checklist below. If you can run code, run `python scripts/check_copy.py your-text.txt` and fix every "problem" it reports.

### Step 8. Reply in this exact order

1. **The new version**, ready to copy and paste.
2. **2 alternative headlines.**
3. **What changed**, in 3 bullets or fewer, in plain words.
4. **Score:** old score and new score out of 30 (from the scorecard).
5. **Proof to collect**, only if you left markers: what, and the exact message to send customers to get it.
6. **One test to try:** a simple, fair way to compare old and new (see [references/TESTING.md](references/TESTING.md)).

Keep everything outside the new version short. The new version is the product; the rest is support.

## Check before you send

- [ ] The first line is a result for the customer, not a description of the business.
- [ ] A stranger understands what they get within 5 seconds.
- [ ] There is a concrete before and after that passes the camera test.
- [ ] Every number, review, client, award and guarantee came from the person. Nothing was invented, nothing rounded up.
- [ ] Missing proof is marked `[PROOF NEEDED: ...]`, never faked.
- [ ] There is exactly one next step, and it says what happens after.
- [ ] No explaining phrases are left (see the list).
- [ ] "You" appears more than "we".
- [ ] Same offer, same prices, same facts, same tone as the original.
- [ ] No outcome promises in health, legal or finance.

## Special situations

- **No proof at all.** Don't stall. Use process proof ("You get a written quote within 24 hours", "Every job ends with before and after photos sent to you"), which is real if they really do it. Mark the rest. Give the 2 week collection plan from the proof rules.
- **Regulated businesses (health, legal, finance, insurance, education claims).** Never promise outcomes ("guaranteed", "cure", "you will win", "returns of X%"). Show the process, qualifications, experience and allowed reviews. Add one line: "Check this wording with your regulator or lawyer before publishing."
- **They ask for fake reviews, made up numbers or fake urgency.** Refuse that part in one sentence ("I can't invent reviews or numbers: it breaks consumer protection rules and platform policies, and it backfires when people check"). Then give the strongest honest version.
- **B2B or very technical products.** The result is still human: the buyer's time saved, money earned, risk avoided, or how they look to their boss. Lead with that; put the technical detail lower, for the people who need it.
- **Many pieces at once.** Do them one at a time: homepage first (it shapes everything else), then the rest. Ask before continuing.
- **They want it shorter or longer.** Keep all 6 rules; change only the length. Shortest useful form: result line, 1 proof line, 1 next step.
- **Another language or a mix (for example Hindi and English).** Write in the language and mix they use. The rules don't change.
- **They only want feedback, not a rewrite.** Give the score, the top 3 problems with quoted lines, and 1 rewritten line per problem as an example.
- **The business really has no clear result yet** (a brand new business). Use the result they intend to deliver, written as a promise they can keep ("Your first draft in 48 hours"), plus process proof. Never fake history.
